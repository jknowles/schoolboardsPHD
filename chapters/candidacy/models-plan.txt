Theories of Formation

Theory 1: Institutional Reform
  - Normative Progressive era perspective
  - Government efficiency and economic growth are the main goals
  - Secondary goal is equity across the metro region (p.29)

* Rests on 2 key assumptions
- 1) Study needs to bof MSA as a whole, not political subjurisdictions
- 2) Welfare of whole > welfare of the parts of the metropolitan region

4 Tenets of Reform
1. Political fragmentation is bad and leads to chaotic / inefficient services, 
due to no economy of scales, bad overall decisions
2. Overlapping government leads to wasteful duplication
3. Overlap leads to citizen confusion, voter apathy, and higher costs. Lack of 
transparency and complexity increases information costs and increases taxes
4. Political fragmentation increases fiscal disparities and inequity. Functional 
specialization in particular like in schools drives up costs. 

Critics of Reform School
- Reformers ignore that voters have consistently rejected monolithic local 
governments
- Reformers view the special purpose governments as all the same, ignore variation in them
- Ignore possibility that special purpose governments create more nuanced bundles of public goods for consumers
- Untested / little evidence that special purpose governments have a negative impact
- "Metropolitan" scale is inconsistent and imprecise -- small and huge metro areas exist, unclear where metro-boundaries are

Theory 2: Public Choice

Competitive market ensures efficient delivery of services. 

- To achieve efficiency in public good, non-market forces such as voting, taxing, 
etc. are necessary
- Tiebout sorting, that citizens vote with their feet about provision of public 
goods
- Differentiates the "provision" of public goods from their "production"
- Small units + specialization is seen as good and efficiency inducing, a better 
match for consumers and decreased marginal cost of new government formation
- U.S. Advisory Commision on Intergovernmental Relations (ACIR) - embraces public 
choice theory, no a prior reason to reduce special puprose governments

Positives:
1) Acknowledges rational reasons citizens might form special purpose governments
2) Economic justirication for special purpose governments enhances efficiency at 
all scales
3) Distinguishes efficiency from equity (p. 40)

Problems:
1) Assumes efficiency is enhanced even when evidence shows that special purpose 
governments are often formed for non-economic motives
2) Boundaries are suboptimal from rational perspective -ignore non-economic factors 
like politics, intergovernmental relations, historical practices
3) Metro areas have not converged on one dominant form of special purpose government, 
huge diversity suggests non-economic forces across regions can inhibit/enhance 
formation
4) Assumes competition - when in fact specialization is a strategy to gain a 
monopoly and avoid competition. Additionally moving costs are high. 
5) Citizen voice (not exit) is also suboptimal due to low visibility of special 
purpose governments, unelected leaders, and non-open meetings

p. 41

Theory 3: Metropolitcan Ecology Perspective
- Conceive of metro areas as places social groups vie for control of territory
- Struggle is political, contentious, tough

4 tenets
1) Reformers are wrong, residents will not and should not give up benefits of small-scale democracy for large bureaucracy and unproven gains in efficiency (jk - what are the benefits of small-scale democracy?)
2) Assume any existing arrangements benefit the status quo, government reorganization 
thus threatens status quo and threatens stability -- to be avoided in favor of 
"muddling through"
3) Political fragmentation is a way of entrenching socioeconomic disparities
4) State government sets rules on local government and these are profoundly important 

On Special Jurisdictions:
- Ambivalent about special purposes governments
- Special jurisdictions may be able to increase revenues and services, but makes them 
vulnerable to special interests
- Result is special purpose government provided services are over-provided and 
this may create bias in favor of capital intensive - revenue producing tasks

Criticisms:
1) Focuses too much on public authorities -- and cannot explain varities even 
within these well
2) Preference for accommodation is blinding. No evidence special purpose governments 
are more accommodating than a general purpose government

p. 47

Theory 4: Structuralist Perspective

Markets and technology explain metropolitan structure, but do not provide a 
motivation for change / development
Government is a prime collaborator with capitalists in urban development
Capitalists however are not united, made of numerous factions with different 
goals for urban development

- View special purpose government as a mechanism for elites to further economic 
and development goals
- Speical purpose governments can bypass citizen preferences because they are 
conceived as manipulable passive institutions
- Evidence of this is that developers often push for creation of special district 
governments, environmental groups rarely get involved
- Special purpose governemtn diversity represents varying goals of capitalist 
factions. Some theorists believe special purpose governments pursue own institutional 
survival through a program of growth/expansion (policy)
  - Often claim they are "outside of politics" to insulate themselves behind 
    technocratic lexicon -- want institutional freedom (jk - so true for schools)
- Also help general purpose government put tough issues through less visible 
groups

Critique:
1) Fail to acknowledge special purpose government formed by citizens and not 
pro-growth (library, recreation), also, citizens can create special jurisdictions 
to pursue a pro-growth agenda
2) Special do pursue anti-growth / moratoria on development policies
3) Methodological assumption that all contexts are unique so no comparisons / 
generalizations are possible across metro-areas
