\section{Introduction}

School boards have considerable power over local issues in their community. We have 
seen that school boards are often responsible for setting property tax levies, 
labor relations with teachers and support staff within the school, and often serve 
as the largest single employer in their community. The driving idea behind locally 
elected school boards was to ensure that the local community retained some control 
over the direction taken by the schools in their community and to ensure that 
the school system was responsive to the preferences of that community. Until 
now there have only been limited tests of school board responsiveness. 

The challenge in testing school board responsiveness is great. First, in most 
cases school boards are non-partisan offices and education issues do not fall 
along traditional ideological lines \citep[for counter example:][]{RefWorks:347}. 
This poses a challenge for researchers seeking evidence of such responsiveness. 
Additionally, the majority of school board election activity is low-impact and 
low-cost, eliminating traditional ways of measuring the information provided to 
voters about candidates and their policy preferences \citep{RefWorks:322, RefWorks:321}. 

Voter policy preferences were expressed and made clear through two elections in 
Wisconsin - the 2010 Gubernatorial election, and updated with the 2011 recall 
election.

\section{School Boards and Policy Change}

The literature on school boards affecting policy change is dominated by a focus on 
three areas - 1) superintendent turnover as policy change, 2) setting tax levies, or 
3) making direct policy changes about social issues, and 4) changes resulting in 
student outcomes. 

Much of the work referred to in Chapter \ref{chap:intro} dealt with the impact 
of school boards on policy change. In fact, both the dissatisfaction theory and the 
public choice theories of school board politics include superintendent turnover 
as key evidence \citep{RefWorks:297,RefWorks:271, RefWorks:351, RefWorks:339,RefWorks:340,RefWorks:243}. 
Recently a key improvement in empirical tests of this work has focused on political 
vs. apolitical turnover, typically retirement, among school board members and 
superintendents \citep{RefWorks:311}. 

There is a strong public economics literature that views school boards as agents 
that set the price for the public good of education through property tax rates. % citations needed

\citet{RefWorks:347} represents the most thorough study of school boards as agents 
of change in social issues. Some view school boards as potentially captured by 
special interest groups \citep{RefWorks:320}. There is evidence that when 
boards are elected, they become more responsive to interest groups than previously 
when they were appointed \citep{RefWorks:242}. 


Boards can influence student outcomes through a range of decisions - some direct 
and some less direct. \citet{RefWorks:210} provides an example by examining the 
relationship between board decisions to consolidate school districts and the 
resulting changes in student achievement. On a more universal basis, \citet{RefWorks:214} 
explores if boards change policies in response to public accountability for student 
achivement, and if this results in improvements in student achievement.\footnote{This 
argument assumes a certain level of effectiveness that is not captured by any 
measures in the current study and may not be evenly distributed across boards \citep{RefWorks:237,RefWorks:261}.} 
In many cases, boards are criticized as being highly constrained actors with 
little room to set policy after complying with state and federal mandates \citep{RefWorks:316}, 
although the extent of this is not clear \citep{RefWorks:223}.\footnote{Note that this 
argument can be reduced down ever further to the impact of district policies on schools 
or school policies on classroom \citep{RefWorks:255}}

Yet boards allocate resources, and resources matter \citep{RefWorks:183}.
