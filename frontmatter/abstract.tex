There are over 13,000 school districts that operate in the United States run 
independently by school boards. They are responsible for annual expenditures 
equal to the budget of the US Department of Defense. They decide on curriculum, 
school construction and closures, enrichment programs, staff compensation and more. 
These decisions ripple through every American community and have implications for
everything from property values and economic development to the quality of 
education provided in each community. Despite this wide reaching scope and the 
importance of these decisions, local school boards remain woefully 
understudied (Land 2002). Limited work has been done on exploring the conditions 
under which voters participate in school board elections and candidates choose 
to run, but never with an eye on evaluating causal relationships.

This dissertation seeks to explore the degree of democratic control communities 
have over school boards. To do this, I build on the theoretical distinction between 
democratic potential and actualized democratic behavior. Using a panel data set 
of Wisconsin school board election records from 2002-2012, I explore how often 
and under what conditions voters and candidates participate in school board 
elections, and what the result of such participation means. 

Wisconsin is selected in part because of the introduction of reforms that greatly 
increased the power of school boards during the period of study. Boards were given
unanticipated freedom in setting employee compensation and work rules, and education
became the premier issue in a bitterly contested statewide series of elections. By
comparing the behavior of candidates, board members, boards, and voters before and 
after the policy shock it is possible to evaluate the causal impact of state 
level policy on the politics of school boards.
