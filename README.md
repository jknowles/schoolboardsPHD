# School Boards and Policy Shocks

### A PhD Dissertation in Political Science

### by Jared E. Knowles

## Introduction

- 13,000 school districts with elected board members in the United States
- Political scientists know that local elections don't conform to state and 
national partisan election theories
- Boards are elected, 50,000+ board members, but little research on the electoral 
process that gets them there
- Board members responsible for overseeing expenditures equal to the U.S. Department 
of Defense
- Responsible (in most cases) for setting the majority of property tax levies
- Scholars have been interested in exploring whether or not school board elections 
are democratic, and have put forth a number of theories

1. Dissatisfaction Theory
  * The canonical theory suggesting that board election activity can be low and 
  still democratic if school boards respond to periodic community dissatisfaction 
2. Continuous Participation Theory
  * Akin to regulatory capture, the board represents the will of the 5-10% of the 
  public engaged in school decision making, and periodic participation and turnover 
  represents their will. 
3. Decision-Output Theory
  * Undemocratic because voters can only determine who makes decisions, but not 
  anything about decisions themselves. 
4. Public Choice Theory
  * Voters participate when a candidate emerges who reflects their interests, 
  their perceived benefits are great enough, and their chance of victory is 
  enough. 
  
In addition to these theoretical underpinnings, a number of studies have surveyed 
school board members in an attempt to understand their electoral environment, 
motivations, and demographics. These studies have relied on candidate self-ratings 
and candidate self-reports for the most part to estimate rates of incumbent 
challenge, incumbent defeat, and levels of fundraising in school boards. 

A further issue is that school districts, like most local governments, are 
substantially constrained policy making entities (Manna, Oliver). As such, it is 
unclear how well voters can assign benefits and costs to the activity of these 
administrative units in the broader array of government agencies in the American 
federalist landscape. Oliver 2012 suggests that local governments are less subject 
to partisanship and local elections reward competent management of public 
services over ideology. 

This dissertation enters into this discussion a number of ways. First, by looking 
at a panel of election results for school districts that spans at a minimum 7 years 
in the state of Wisconsin, it represents the first time it is possible to 
describe the official rates of school board election, incumbency rates, margins 
of victory, and incumbent defeat in a local-control environment. This allows 
for the exploration of many political science findings about the influence of 
demographics, partisanship, and policy-performance on rates of voter turnout and 
candidate participation in elections. 

Second, during the period of study, Wisconsin experienced a dramatic state-level 
statewide policy shock to education policy with the passage of Wisconsin Act 10 
and the dissolution of public employee unions. Wisconsin Act 10 represented a large 
transfer of power away from the collective bargaining framework between districts 
and employees into the hands of school districts, and their elected caretakers -- 
school board members. Thus the elections following the passage of this law 
represent a period in which the policy advantages of electing school board 
members has been made clear through the historic controversy surrounding this 
move. In the period immediately following the passage of Act 10 school districts 
were able to completely renegotiate their compensation packages and their staffing 
in ways unavailable to them for decades. 

Thus, the introduction of Act 10 into this series of elections allows for a 
quasi-experimental design to test the causal impact of a policy shift at the 
state level on electoral activity at the local level. As an added benefit, this 
quasi-experimental is expected to be mediated by the community support for or 
against the policy of Scott Walker -- something that is directly measured by the 
two elections Scott Walker faced immediately before and after the passage of Act 10. 

To test the applicability of political science theory at the local level I gather 
data on election results from 75% of Wisconsin school districts. The majority 
of records are available for a five year period between 2007 and 2012, with a 
smaller subset providing records from 2002-2012. These records are then paired 
with an array of administrative data on school districts available from the 
Wisconsin Department of Public Instruction including data on the student population, 
adult population, income levels, school performance, finances, and political 
leanings of the school district and the population it serves. Combining this 
data allows for an array of theories to be tested on the predictors of when 
school board races are challenged and when voters turnout for school boards.

## Why Run for School Board?

The literature on school board members suggest that school board members are older, 
wealthier, better educated, whiter, and more conservative than the communities 
that they serve. This is in line with the general literature on candidates for 
local office (Oliver). School board races are often uncontested even when 
no incumbent is running for office. School board races are, however, contested 
and almost all school districts will experience a contested race over a multiple 
year period. 

The puzzle for school boards is what motivates candidates to run? As Oliver and 
Hess both indicate, local office is rarely a stepping stone or a prestigous  
post. There are no partisan stepping ladders to higher office. There is little 
to no pay. In fact, it is a surprise that in communities with fewer than 1,000 
adults over 18, there are more than two individuals interested in such a position. 
In addition, school board service is often unpaid, and in the previous research 
the fact that the most common way to lose a school board seat is to simply 
retire from office has been found to be a confounder to the study of the politics 
of school boards (Alsbury). 

Political scientists have three broad theories about the motivations of candidates 
for office: 

1. Strategic Candidates
2. Political Psychology
3. Policy Preferences

These three theories are not mutually exclusive, but rather attempt to use different 
approaches to understand the question of why candidates choose to run, and the 
further question of what this means for the pool of potential candidates for an 
office. In addition to these three theories there have been a handful of studies 
covering the motivations of those serving in local office such as county board, 
mayor, and school board. 

Strategic candidacy has provided some predictive ability in determining when challengers 
rise to face incumbents in US House and gubernatorial elections. However, the literature 
has struggled with the concern that supposedly amateur candidates seem to follow a 
pattern of strategic entry. Further complicating things is the reliance on the theory 
that more qualified candidates should exhibit more strategic entry, but defining 
challenger quality remains difficult. These problems are exacerbated in local 
settings where challenger quality is difficult to determine in a non-partisan 
managerial setting. 

Political ambition has been part of the political psychology literature suggesting 
that certain psychological traits make individuals more pre-disposed to seeking 
out elected office. The chief puzzle this literature seeks to explain is why only 
some of the individuals with the time, money, and civic skills to devote to running 
for office choose to run (Verba 1995). This literature identifies a number of 
personal factors beyond opportunity and resources that may increase the likelihood 
of seeking office -- ambition, partisan exposure, childhood experiences, and 
individual perceptions of self-efficacy. While many of these measures are unavailable 
in the present study which relies on administrative records, they nevertheless provide 
a basis for selecting demographic characteristics which may be related to a deeper 
pool of potential board candidates such as higher levels of wealth, education, 
and political activity in the community. 

Running for office is often linked to specific policy goals. Studies of political 
amateurs has found that one motivation to seek office is to make specific 
policy changes (Canon 1990). Other studies of candidates have found that policy 
goals have less explanatory power than other factors such as opportunity and 
self-assessment (Fox and Lawless 2005). Specific evidence in the case of school 
boards has found that policy goals can often be a motivating factor in seeking 
that office (Deckman 1999, 2006, Moe). 

Some evidence suggests that candidates in school board elections are either 
motivated by a general sense of public service, or by a specific wish to make 
policy change. We can test these different theories directly using an array 
of district-level attributes to predict the emergence of new candidates or the 
emergence of more candidates than seats (a challenge to the board). 

Dependent variables, at the school district - year level:

* Number of candidates > number of seats
* Number of new candidates (non-repeat, non-incumbents)
* Number of non-incumbent candidates
* Probability of a new candidate (serious) emerging?

Independent variables: 

* Population size
* Percent bachelor or above
* Percent of population non-white
* Percent of population over 65
* Percent of housing that is owner-occupied
* Median income
* Percent of students attending non-public schools
* Percent of public school students non-white
* Percent of public school students low-income
* Election turnout in previous fall on-cycle election (president or gubernatorial)
* Two party vote share for Democrat in previous fall election
* Partisan polarization in previous fall election
* Superintendent turnover
* School referenda election attempt
* School referenda election success
* School district fund balance (cash on hand) per student
* Property value per student (measure of school district taxable value)
* Many more...

The analysis strategy seeks to explore whether the emergence of candidates is 
sensitive to demographic conditions, political conditions, financial conditions, 
or policy conditions. In this vein, the available analyses can be grouped into 
three categories:

Demographics: 

* Population size
* Percent bachelor or above
* Percent of population non-white
* Percent of population over 65
* Percent of housing that is owner-occupied
* Median income
* Percent of students attending non-public schools
* Percent of public school students non-white
* Percent of public school students low-income

These last three also represent a good operationalization of what Oliver 2012 refers 
to as "bias" in the provision of public goods -- that is, how narrowly are the 
benefits provided by a governmental entity within the population. The more narrow 
the scope of the provision of benefits, the less prestigious and attractive the 
office is to ambitious office seekers. In communities where the public schools 
serve a narrower portion of the population because of the availability of private 
options, or where the community served by public schools differs demographically 
from the population candidates are drawn from, school board service may be lower. 

Politics:

* Election turnout in previous fall on-cycle election (president or gubernatorial)
* Two party vote share for Democrat in previous fall election
* Partisan polarization in previous fall election

An important consideration is that serving on a school board may be more reflective 
of a sense of civic duty than of political ambition. Estimating the "civic" nature 
of the candidate pool in a school district is difficult to do, but some levels of 
school district specific political activity are available. These include both the 
political participation rate - voter turnout - in major elections, as well as the 
partisan preferences of the school board electorate. School boards are expected 
to be non-partisan, but partisan division in a community may lead to more political 
conflict at all levels of government, including school boards. 

Policy: 

* Superintendent turnover
* School referenda election attempt
* School referenda election success
* School district fund balance (cash on hand) per student
* Property value per student (measure of school district taxable value)
* Student test scores
* Graduation rates

Dissatisfaction theory and public choice theory (Iaccone and Lutz, Rada, Wu) 
suggest that candidates emerge when policy shocks occur that push school district 
policy away from the preferences of the community. Furthermore, Oliver (2012) finds 
that voters in local off-cycle elections are much more retrospective than voters 
in national or state elections, punishing incumbents for mismanagement or poor 
stewardship of public services. While the array of school policies that could 
lead to community dissatisfaction is too large to parametrize using administrative 
data (the only school board recall in Wisconsin during this time period was the 
result of a disagreement about the cost of a bus purchase and transportation 
routes), several of the larger potential policy shocks do provide data that can be 
used to test their impact on school district candidate emergence. The available 
policy shocks to test include superintendent turnover, failed or attempted increases 
in property tax rates for new construction or to override state revenue limits, 
changes in the district fiscal health as measured by available savings, 
the property wealth of the school district, and finally a diverse array of student 
performance metrics. 




## Why Vote for School Board?

In Wisconsin, school board elections are held during the non-partisan spring 
election cycle. This results in a lower voter turnout (noted by others). A 
key question in the school board literature, much like the broader political 
science literature, is why do voters vote, and what does that decision tell 
us about the electorate and their preferences? While there is not enough data 
to explore this fully, this chapter seeks to explore some basic theories 
of voter turnout in Wisconsin school district elections and how well they 
explain the decision to cast a vote in school board elections.
